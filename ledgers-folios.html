The Ledgers & Folios

Every travel company has a brochure. We kept a book. Here is how to read it — what the numbers mean, why some lines are in pencil, and why James keeps crossing things out.

00

Why a ledger

The short version

A brochure tells you what a place would like you to believe. A ledger tells you what someone actually found there, and when.

Accountants keep ledgers because memory flatters and paper doesn’t. James has kept one, one way or another, since 1982: where he slept, who ran the place, whether he went back. The Ledgers are that book, finally written down properly.

The Folios are the other half. Your trip gets its own number, the house travels with you under it, and when you’re home it goes on your shelf. The Ledger is where we’ve been. Your Folio is where you went.

1 The number
The line on the page. Places are entered in the order they earn their way in, not the order they paid.
2 The ledger ID
Issued once, never recycled. Advisors quote by it, the guides cite it, and your Folio remembers it. If a house changes its name, the number doesn’t.
3 The line beneath
Every room, cabin, day or stage is entered under its parent, with its own sub-number. A lodge is not a room. A voyage is not a day. The book knows the difference.
4 The stamp
James’s mark, stamped when he files it, with the date. Six marks, below. None can be bought, none are guessed.
5 The pencil
In the library, not yet marked. Most of the book is still in pencil — we’d rather show you that than pretend otherwise.
6 The margin
James’s note, in his hand. Usually about time: which night is the good one, when to arrive, what to skip.
7 The red pen
Where the brochure crept in, he crosses it out and writes what’s actually there, and initials it. Adjectives are the first thing to go.
8 The seal
Pressed once, where the page is carried forward. It means the page was checked by a person, top to bottom. One seal per page; it isn’t confetti.

The top of the book. James stayed, and he’ll tell you which room and which night was the good one.

I’ve Walked ItInspected in person.

He walked the rooms, ate the food and asked the awkward questions. He didn’t sleep there. Yet.

I Know the HostThe house will patch you in.

He has met the person who runs it. If something needs fixing at midnight, there is a name, not a switchboard.

I Keep Going BackA founder favorite.

Not a favorite. A habit. The places he returns to with his own money and his own family.

I Know Who KnowsVetted by a partner.

Somewhere he hasn’t been, inspected by someone he would trust with his own trip.

I’ll Know SoonOn my list, not yet in my bed.

Pencilled. In the library, not yet earned. We tell you so, which is more than most.

Why the page looks like this

Three drafts, one verdict

We drew the ledger three times before it looked like the truth. In the interest of being forthright, here are all three.

Draft one · The illustrated ledger

Photographs on every line

A framed picture beside each entry, photo corners and all. It was lovely. It was also a brochure wearing a ledger’s coat — the eye went to the pool, not the proof.

pretty. not honest enough.

See draft one →

Draft two · The ledger by hand

Text only, ruled like an account book

No pictures, no adjectives. The house on one line, its rooms beneath it, the number down the side. Suddenly you could read a whole collection at a glance — and see what was missing.

better. too quiet.

See draft two →

Draft three · The inked ledger

The same page, worked on

Stamps where James has filed a mark, his notes in the margin, the red pen where the brochure crept in, the seal where the page is checked. The data stays in clean type underneath; the ink is the person who checked it.

this one. J.W.

The Folio

Your page of the book

Every trip gets a number, the house travels with you under it, and afterwards it goes on your shelf — not into your camera roll.

Your Folio carries the ledger numbers of every stay, cabin and day you booked, the notes you made along the way, and the host who answered when you called. It’s private unless you share it with your advisor. Open it in ten years; it will still know where you had lunch.
