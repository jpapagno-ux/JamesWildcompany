    04 · RULES OF THE ROAD

    Forty-four years of voyages, boiled down

    The design theories behind every Wild & Co voyage, in James's words.

    RULE 01

    The smaller the boat, the bigger the sea.

    Sixteen guests means first ashore, last back, and a naturalist who learns your name before the pangas do.

    RULE 02

    Read a place in the order it was written.

    Old islands first, young ones last. It's geology, and it's better storytelling than any brochure.

    RULE 03

    Arrive the day before the day before.

    Mainland flights are late more often than ships are. A night in Guayaquil or Quito is cheap insurance.

    RULE 04

    Never trust a brochure with no weather in it.

    June to November is cool, misty and alive with marine life; December to May is warm with calmer seas. Both are good. Neither is "always sunny".

    RULE 05

    Contrast beats coverage.

    Pair the sea with a few days of land, in the Andes or the Santa Cruz highlands. You'll notice the islands more for having left them.

    I've struck "luxury cruise" out of the ledger. It's a yacht with sixteen guests and a naturalist who knows every sea lion by its scars. Call it what you like.
    — J. Wild

    bucket list — The Galápagos isn't a box to tick. It's the one place people come back from talking about evolution at dinner parties, which is worse, and better.

    09 · STRAIGHT ANSWERS
    Questions people ask before they book
    Which Galápagos Sea Star route should I choose?
    Cruise A (Tuesday to Sunday) covers the old southern and central islands, Española and Floreana. Cruise B (Sunday to Thursday) goes north to Genovesa and Bartholomew. Cruise C (Thursday to Tuesday) heads west to Isabela and Fernandina. For a first visit with time, the 15-day Full Circle joins all three without changing suites.
    How many guests does the Galápagos Sea Star carry?
    Sixteen guests in eight suites: one 35 m² Sea Star Suite, three 25 m² Galápagos Suites on the upper deck and four 25 m² Balcony Suites on the main deck, with ten crew, a cruise director and a bilingual naturalist.
    Can I charter the whole Galápagos Sea Star?
    Yes. All eight suites can be chartered for up to sixteen guests on any route, including the Full Circle. Meal times and celebrations are yours; visitor sites still follow National Park permits.
    Is a small-ship Galápagos voyage good for solo travelers?
    Yes. With sixteen guests you'll know everyone by the second day. Ask about the single supplement or a shared suite.
    When is the best time to visit the Galápagos?
    There's no bad month. December to May is warmer with calmer seas; June to November is cooler and misty with the richest marine life. Waved albatross nest on Española from about April to December.
    What isn't included in a Galápagos voyage?
    Usually flights from Guayaquil or Quito, the Galápagos National Park entrance fee, the Transit Control Card, gratuities and travel insurance. Confirm current fees before you travel.
    
  
    MARKETING DIRECTOR'S REPORT · SEO & AEO
    Building the voyage template to rank and to be quoted
    

    This page is the master template for every Wild & Co voyage. Each block maps to a search intent and to a question AI engines get asked: route, ship, who it suits, what it isn't, when to go, what it costs, and charter. The map, the day-by-day text and the structured data all come from one itinerary file, so they can't disagree.
    

    Design notes · the zoom and the day-by-day
      
 
    • Zoom by day, not by stop. The chart frames all of a day's stops at once and holds still while the boat, plane or 4×4 moves inside the frame. Between days it pulls back (further for flights) and dives into the next day. That rhythm (wide, close, wide, close) is what makes the itinerary readable.
      • 
 
    • Globe first. Every route opens on the turning globe, then flies down to the region and lands on day one, the same opening as the ARC X and TERRA Y charts. It tells the guest where in the world they're going before it tells them anything else.
      • 
 
    • Keep the map simple. Land and water only, engraved: hatching along the inside of each coast, wave lines along the outside, a graticule and a compass rose. Lakes get the same treatment as the sea. Nothing on the map competes with the route.
      • 
 
    • One symbol for the vehicle. A small ink roundel shows how each leg travels (plane, 4×4, on foot, boat or ship) and points the way it's going. The route line changes style with it: dotted for flights, solid for road, dashed for water.
      • 
 
    • The chart is the entrance. Each trip opens on a near full-screen chart with the route title laid over it. Controls sit directly under the chart, then a "Today on the chart" panel that changes as the trip plays, then the full day-by-day details. Guests see the journey before they read about it.
      • 
 
    • Zoom in on the moving leg. On every leg the camera closes in on the vehicle (yacht, plane, helicopter, 4×4, walking party or dhow) and pulls back only as far as a long leg needs. On arrival it eases out to frame the day. At the deepest zoom the vehicles are drawn as engraved plates: a wake behind the yacht, the plane's shadow on the sea, dust behind the 4×4, footprints behind the walkers.
      • 
 
    • Day card typography. Day number in a typewriter roundel; the day's title in Playfair Display at about 30px; stops as a timeline (time and mode in Courier caps, place in Cormorant semibold, coordinates in small Courier); body text in EB Garamond at 16–17px, capped near 62 characters; James's note in Caveat, signed. One accent colour per volume carries the active day.
      • 
 
    • Photo strip per day. A horizontal, snap-scrolling strip of three or four images at 4:3, one per stop plus one "life aboard" or "evening in camp" frame, so each day shows place, wildlife and how it feels.
